What Cloud Computing Really Is

The cloud isn't just “someone else's server” rented by the month. It's a way of delivering resources: self-service, elastic and billed by usage.

Cloud computing is a model for delivering computing resources (servers, storage, networking and software) over the Internet on a pay-as-you-go basis. Instead of buying and running your own hardware, you rent exactly the resources you need, for exactly as long as you need them.

What actually counts as “cloud”?

The most widely used definition comes from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST SP 800-145). A system is only truly cloud when it has all five characteristics:

  • On-demand self-service: users create servers and disks themselves, without waiting for someone to approve a ticket.
  • Broad network access: usable from a browser, a phone, the command line or an API.
  • Resource pooling: many customers share the same physical infrastructure while staying isolated from each other.
  • Rapid elasticity: scale up or down in minutes, often automatically based on load.
  • Measured service: every bit of usage is metered, which is the basis for billing and optimization.

Quick testA fully virtualized server room where you still email IT to get a VM is not a cloud yet: it's missing self-service.

Three service models: IaaS, PaaS, SaaS

The three models differ in who is responsible for which layer of the stack. Moving from IaaS to SaaS, the provider takes on more and you control less.

Model Provider manages You manage Examples
IaaS Hardware, network, virtualization OS, runtime, application, data Amazon EC2, Google Compute Engine
PaaS Also the OS and runtime Application code and data Google App Engine, Heroku
SaaS Everything, including the app Only your data and how you use it Gmail, Google Docs, Microsoft 365

A handy analogy is pizza: IaaS is renting a kitchen and baking from scratch, PaaS is buying a ready-made base and adding your own toppings, and SaaS is ordering delivery.

Four deployment models

Public cloud

Infrastructure owned by a provider and shared by many customers over the Internet. Fast to start, with no upfront investment.

Private cloud

Infrastructure dedicated to one organization, on-premises or hosted. High control and a good fit for sensitive data, but you carry the operating cost.

Hybrid cloud

A mix of public and private: critical data stays in-house while traffic spikes burst out to the public cloud.

Community cloud

A group of organizations with shared requirements, such as hospitals or universities, share one infrastructure.

Benefits and trade-offs

  • CapEx becomes OpEx: no hardware to buy upfront, just a monthly operating cost.
  • Speed: spin up a test environment in minutes instead of weeks.
  • High availability: running across multiple regions and data centers is straightforward.

In return, you have to plan for vendor lock-in, costs that are hard to predict without monitoring, and the shared responsibility model: the provider secures the infrastructure, but a misconfiguration in your account is on you.
